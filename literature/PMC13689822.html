Analysis of Multiple Tire Rubber-Derived Chemicals in Fish Bile and Plasma: Analytical Method and Field Assessment in Puget Sound, WA, USA

Denis da Silva, Jonelle Gates, Madison Hattaway, Louisa Harding, Sandra O’Neill, Irvin Schultz

Environmental Science & Technology · 2026 · doi:10.1021/acs.est.6c07136 · PMC13689822

Abstract

Tire rubber-derived chemicals (TRCs) are known to be highly toxic to fish, but understanding of their environmental levels and fate is still limited. This study presents a new method for the analysis of 18 TRCs, including phenylenediamines (TRC-PDs), their quinone derivatives (TRC-Qs), and rubber vulcanizing and curing-related chemicals (TRC-VCRs) in fish bile and plasma samples. We applied this method in bile and plasma samples from English sole collected in urban, near-urban, and nonurban sites in Puget Sound, WA, USA. TRCs were detected in 100% of bile and 96% of plasma samples from English sole collected from all nine sites in Puget Sound. Mean TRC levels in bile samples per site ranged between 0.8 and 18 ng/mL, while plasma levels ranged between 0.22 and 0.63 ng/mL, with total summed reaching up to 31 ng/mL in bile and 1.3 ng/mL in plasma. TRC-PDs were the most abundant group of TRC in both bile and plasma. Higher levels of TRCs in bile suggests this matrix to be more valuable for environmental monitoring, providing more sensitive detection of TRC during low exposures. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first data set reporting environmental levels of 18 TRCs in the bile and plasma of field-caught fish.

Keywords: tire chemicals, TRCs, 6PPD-Q, 6PPD, Puget Sound, English sole, bile, plasma

1. Introduction

Tire wear particles are a major component of the stormwater mixture. Following exposure of fish to tire rubber leachates, various harmful effects have been reported, including impacts on early life stage growth and development, survival rates, fertilization, and other end points. − During the manufacturing process of vehicle tires, a blend of synthetic and natural rubber is combined with various chemicals, including antioxidants, vulcanizing accelerators, and rubber curing and cross-linking agents, which collectively constitute 10–14% of the final tire material. , The environmental levels and fate of the majority of these tire rubber-derived chemicals (TRCs), including their transformation products, are still poorly understood worldwide. −

The tire rubber antioxidant called 6PPD [N-(1,3-dimethylbutyl)-N′-phenyl–phenylenediamine] is widely present in urban stormwater runoff. Research has linked a quinone transformation product of 6PPD with acute prespawn mortality of coho salmon (Oncorhynchus kisutch). This finding has increased efforts to develop analytical methods for measuring 6PPD, its quinone derivative 6PPD-Q and other TRCs in surface water, − and fish. , Additionally, it has promoted research focused on determining the toxicity of 6PPD-Q and stormwater in various fish species. −

Multiple TRCs have been detected in environmental samples such as water, sediment, air, soil, and wastewater effluents, − indicating these chemicals are ubiquitous in the environment. Limited field data on multiple TRC levels have been reported in biota. ,, There is a significant deficiency in the current literature regarding methods and field assessments for a broader range of TRCs. Specifically, there is a notable lack of research on multi-TRCs in fish from urbanized coastal areas of the United States. To overcome this critical gap, we developed a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS)-based method that is rapid, robust, and highly sensitive for the simultaneous analysis of 18 TRCs in fish bile and plasma. We applied this new method to bile and plasma samples from 80 adult English sole (Parophrys vetulus) collected throughout Puget Sound, WA, USA.

2. Materials and Methods

A detailed description of all reagents, method development, laboratory validation tests, the final extraction protocol, quality control, statistical analysis, instrument conditions, and field bile and plasma sampling can be found in the Supporting Information.

2.1. Area of Study and Environmental Samples

Puget Sound is an urbanized fjord-like enclosed estuary where the city of Seattle is located, which has a population that ranked in the top ten for urban growth in the USA in 2024. This area is a gateway for several anadromous salmonids when returning to spawn in freshwater, including Chinook (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha), chum (Oncorhynchus keta), bull trout (Salvelinus confluentus), and steelhead trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), all listed as threatened under the US Endangered Species Act. In addition, Puget Sound serves as the critical habitat of the endangered ESA-listed southern resident killer whales (Orcinus orca), among other marine mammals protected under the Marine Mammal Protection Act.

English sole were collected from several sites within Puget Sound, WA, USA, by the Washington State Department of Fish and Wildlife (WDFW), as part of a long-term contaminant monitoring program. Sites sampled represent different levels of anthropogenic influence to the adjacent estuarine habitat and are preclassified as urban, near-urban, and nonurban. Details on this classification can be found in the Supporting Information and in other studies. , Nine sites were used in this study and are depicted as represented in Figure. The four urban sites were Elliott Bay/Seattle waterfront (EB), Duwamish River (DU), Commencement Bay (CB), and Sinclair Inlet (SI). Eagle Harbor (EH) was chosen as a near-urban site. The four nonurban sites were Carr Inlet (CIM), Point Madison (PM), Nisqually Reach (NQ), and Hood Canal (HC).

For this study, we utilized bile and plasma of 80 individual male and female adult fish. Each of the nine sites had 6–10 individual fish collected as field replicates and stored at −80 °C until laboratory extraction for TRCs was conducted. English sole sampling and handling were conducted by WDFW staff, as authorized under state lawRevised Code of Washington (RCW chapter 77.12).

2.2. Reagents and Standards

Among the TRCs measured in this study, we selected eight rubber antioxidant phenyl–phenylenediamines and phenylenediamines (TRC-PDs), seven of their quinone transformation products (TRC-Qs), and three vulcanization and curing-related chemicals (TRC-VCRs). A mixture of deuterated TRC-PD and TRC-Q standards was used as extracted surrogate standards (TRC-Sur) to quantify all targeted TRCs. One nonextracted internal standard (TRC-IS) was used to measure the recovery of TRC-Sur. Individual standard manufacturers/sources, as well as purity levels, are provided in Table 1S. Many of the target chemicals in this study have multiple industrial applications. For simplicity, we group them all as TRCs.

2.3. Analytical Method

Briefly, each bile or plasma sample was diluted with water, spiked with TRC-Sur, and extracted via solid-phase extraction (SPE) using a 30 mg reversed-phase cartridge (Strata-X, Phenomenex). Samples were diluted, spiked with TRC-Sur standards, and loaded into SPE cartridges. The matrix-interfering substances were washed off with methanol/water 65/35 (v/v). The target analytes were then eluted with methanol/methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) 60/40 (v/v). MTBE was evaporated from the extract under N2, followed by the addition of TRC-IS. The final methanolic extract was analyzed via LC–MS/MS. The LC unit was equipped with a 100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm particle size, reverse-phased column (Kinetex Coreshell Evo C18, Phenomenex). The LC gradient is provided on Table 2S. A multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM) method was used during data acquisition in the MS/MS analysis for identifying and quantifying each TRC analyte. For final quantitation, each TRC had a response factor generated by a calibration curve with a minimum of five concentration points. Individual limits of quantitation (LOQ) for each TRC are given in Table 3S. All individual MRM ions (m/z) monitored are provided in Table.

2.4. Method Validation

To develop and validate the analytical method, we used coho salmon bile and sablefish (Anoplopoma fimbria) plasma as test samples. These samples were not intentionally exposed to the chemicals of interest in this study as test samples. Several spike blanks (LC–MS-grade water) and test bile and plasma samples were used to optimize each extraction parameter. Extraction efficiency (prespiked) tests, where a mixture of all TRCs was spiked directly into the test samples and then processed, were conducted to evaluate the overall recovery efficiency of the method. Matrix effect (postspiked) tests, where the TRC mixture was spiked into the final extract, were used to assess potential matrix effects such as suppression or enhancement of each analyte in the final LC–MS/MS analysis. The final protocol for SPE extraction was validated in triplicate using both test samples and blanks, pre- and postspiked with low (0.025–1.5 ng/mL, depending on instrument sensitivity for each TRC) and high (5 ng/mL) spike levels.

3. Results and Discussion

3.1. Method Validation

Both fish bile and plasma are important biofluids and are known to be reliable matrices for investigating the exposure of fish to anthropogenic contaminants. Bile typically contains higher concentrations of less lipophilic contaminants and their metabolites compared to tissues. − Plasma is also an important fluid for analysis, as it provides information on bioactive contaminant levels and can be used to estimate toxicity levels based on toxicokinetic models. , Additionally, both matrices usually require minimal sample treatment, which allows for more rapid analysis.

Table shows the mean recoveries obtained for the method validation for all of the target TRCs. Figure 1S shows the results from the method validation tests, including individual replicates for each test. The method validation and field sample analysis in this study adhere to the guidelines of our established analytical QAP for other LC–MS/MS analyses.

Overall, the mean percent recovery (±standard deviation, SD) for the prespiked test for blanks (water), bile, and plasma were 85 ± 16%, 83 ± 6%, and 84 ± 14%, respectively. While the reversed-phase chromatography system (both SPE and LC instrument) generally favors more polar TRCs, such as the quinone transformation products (TRC-Qs) and TRC-VCRs, most of the antioxidant TRC-PDs presented relatively lower extraction efficiencies. However, their extraction efficiencies were considered acceptable given the complexity of the analyte list.

The mean percent recovery (±SD) for the postspiked test for all of the TRCs and both spike levels was 108 ± 7% for blanks, 91 ± 5% for bile, and 106 ± 11% for plasma. These results demonstrate that this extraction method is capable of removing most interfering substances from both plasma and bile samples.

3.2. 6PPD and 7PPD Calibration Adjustment

Both 6PPD and 7PPD standards used for the calibration curve had one of their oxidative transformation products detected, 6QDI (N-1,3-dimethyl-butyl-N′-phenyl quinone diamine) and 7QDI (N-1,4-dimethyl-pentyl-N′-phenyl quinone diamine), respectively. These transformation products were detected in the individual stock solutions prepared in methanol and all subsequent dilutions for the calibration curve (data not shown). This may be due to standard impurity (purities were above 96%, see Table 1S), rapid degradation once in solution, or postsynthesis/storage transformation. 6QDI and 7QDI were, respectively, present in the 6PPD and 7PPD standards at 40% of the intensity of the peak corresponded to 6PPD and 7PPD in all calibration levels. The transitions (precursor/product ions, m/z) monitored for 6QDI and 7QDI were 267.0/183.1 and 280.5/183.1, respectively, and were determined with the use of their pure standards infused in the mass spectrometer. We presented the 6PPD and 7PPD concentrations corrected by a 0.6 factor (or 60% of original calculated concentration) due to the presence of these transformation products in the standards. However, the stability test showed that concentrations of both 6PPD and 7PPD are stable once kept in 100% methanol and stored at −20 °C for at least five months (see Figure 2SA), strongly suggesting a standard contamination or postsynthesis/storage partial degradation. The transformation of 6PPD into 6QDI as part of the quinone transformation route has been proposed. The potential presence of QDIs in 6PPD and 7PPD standards should be verified, and calibration curve adjustment should be applied in other studies reporting concentrations of 6PPD or 7PPD. To our knowledge, previous studies have neither reported the occurrence of QDI transformation products as cross-interfering chemicals in their standard nor investigated transformation products present in their original stocks and calibration curve solutions.

3.3. Analysis of TRCs in English Sole from Puget Sound, WA

3.3.1. Field Distribution of TRCs in English Sole Bile and Plasma

The total summed TRC concentrations (summed TRCs = TRC-PDs + TRC-Qs + TRC-VCRs) reached 31 ng/mL in bile and 1.3 ng/mL in plasma. We observed a high detection rate in English sole samples: 100% of the bile and 96% of the plasma samples in this study had at least one TRC detected. Figure illustrates the mean summed TRC concentration per site type for both bile and plasma samples. For both bile and plasma, the mean summed TRCs for urban sites was significantly different from nonurban sites (p < 0.0001 for bile and 0.0185 for plasma). The mean summed TRCs (±SD ng/mL) for urban, near-urban, and nonurban sites were, respectively, 9.4 ng/mL (±7.3), 6.4 ng/mL (±2.2), and 1.7 ng/mL (±1.5) for bile and 0.52 ng/mL (±0.16), 0.55 ng/mL (±0.29), and 0.38 ng/mL (±0.23) ng/mL for plasma, calculated from each individual sample. Urbanized areas, which have greater amounts of impervious ground cover and higher volume of vehicle circulation, tend to generate a higher volume of stormwater runoff. This urban runoff typically contains a greater concentration of tire wear particles and TRCs that ultimately reach aquatic systems. The same pattern of spatial distribution of TRCs has been reported in sediment from Pearl River Delta, South China Sea, where higher concentrations of summed TRCs were found in urban river regions and lower levels in more remote areas, such as coastal and deep-sea sediments. While there are limited studies on multi-TRC levels in fish samples from different urbanization levels, the comparison with these sediment samples is relevant to our study. As a benthic fish species, English sole is highly exposed to sediment contaminants.

TableA–B summarizes the mean concentrations of individual TRCs measured in English sole bile (A) and plasma (B) in this study for each site in Puget Sound. The highest mean summed TRCs per site were measured in the bile samples of fish caught in the urban sites DU (18 ng/mL) and CB (12 ng/mL). Both of these sites are known to have high levels of anthropogenic contaminants, due to the intense industrial activities and urbanization levels. ,, All nonurban sites had relatively low levels of mean summed TRCs. In plasma samples, the mean summed TRC was also relatively higher for urban sites EB (0.63 ng/mL) and DU (0.52 ng/mL) when compared to the nonurban sites CIM (0.24 ng/mL), NQ (0.22 ng/mL), and HC (0.34 ng/mL) but similar to the nonurban PM site (0.63 ng/mL). TRC-PDs were the predominant group of chemicals detected in individual bile and plasma samples at all sites, with the relative percentage ranging from 32 to 97% of the total summed TRCs for bile samples and 15–99% for plasma samples. While TRC-VCRs were the second most abundant group for individual bile samples for most of the sites, with a range of 2.6–53% of the total summed TRCs, individual plasma samples showed a relatively higher percentage of TRC-Qs (4.6–63%). Summed TRC-PDs levels in individual samples were 9.0–32 times higher than summed TRC-Q levels in bile samples from urban sites and 3.7–28 times higher in nonurban site samples. However, the TRC-PDs/TRC-Qs ratio in plasma samples from the same individuals was less prominent, ranging from 0.7 to 9.4 across all plasma samples. A similar distribution of TRCs in sediment samples has been observed in the Pearl River Delta, China, where TRC-PDs were higher than TRC-Qs (TRC-PDs/TRC-Qs = 2.7). Meanwhile, studies measuring TRCs in stormwater runoff or wastewater treatment plant influent and effluent found the total levels of TRC-PDs around 10-fold lower than TRC-Qs. A more balanced distribution between these two TRC classes was observed in air particles and roadside soil samples. In fish muscle collected from the Pearl River Delta (China), the TCR-PDs/TRC-Qs ratio varied substantially, ranging from 0.3 to22, potentially due to differences in the environmental load in their feeding habitat.

In addition to the potential higher levels of TRC-PDs in sediments, as observed in other urbanized areas, an apparent faster metabolism of the quinone derivatives by fish could explain the lower levels of TRC-Qs in fish bile and plasma. Variability in the distribution of TRCs in different types of samples (abiotic and biological matrices) is expected due to their chemical stability in each sample type as well as their susceptibility to biological transformations. Physicochemical properties, metabolism, and toxicokinetic behavior are also factors to be considered when assessing these distributions, particularly when comparing abiotic matrices (sediment or water) with fish tissues (muscle) and biofluids (bile and plasma) or different matrices within the same organism. It has been proposed that the formation of quinones is a part of the metabolic pathway for phenylenediamines, such as 6PPD, and rates of metabolic elimination of quinones and phenylenediamines are species-specific. In vitro metabolism using three different liver microsomes (humans, rats, and fish) was reported to affect a greater decrease in concentration of 6PPD-Q than 6PPD.

Even though TRC-Qs are known to be transformation products of TRC-PDs, individual English sole bile and plasma samples showed no correlation between TRC-PD and TRC-Q levels in all sites (Figure 6S). This lack of correlation in bile and plasma concentrations could be due to many factors, including differences in exposure and bioavailability, environmental degradation, or kinetics of transformation after the absorption of the TRCs.

Based on discriminant analysis, there was a very distinctive pattern and separation of individual TRCs among urban, near-urban, and nonurban sites (Figure) in both bile (A) and plasma (B) samples. Such site separation demonstrates that this method of analysis is selective and sensitive enough to distinguish the differences in TRC levels among sites with different urbanization levels. Among both bile and plasma samples, 6PPD and HMMM were the predominant TRCs responsible for site separation. Even with a known short half-life in the environment, 6PPD was the most important TRC for site separation in both sample types and had very high detection rates in samples from all sites. Table 4S shows the score summary and site classification based on the linear discriminant analysis. After canonical site separation by the bile TRC distribution, only 10% of the bile samples were misclassified among the site types. Plasma TRC separation of sites had 30% of samples misclassified, as plasma levels were slightly less different among sites (Table 4S).

The absence of studies on TRCs in the plasma and bile samples of fish species and in different regions limits comparison with this study. Most TRC studies monitoring fish tissues (muscle or whole body) have not detected both 6PPD and 6PPD-Q in the same sample. , However, one study using whole-body samples of several fish species reported levels that correspond to the 6PPD/6PPD-Q ratio between 0.02 and 0.3 for pelagic species, compared to 175–231 for a benthic fish. This illustrates how the exposure to these chemicals varies based on habitats, where 6PPD-Q is likely more bioavailable through water than via sediment exposure.

Conducting laboratory exposure studies to determine the kinetic differences in absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion between 6PPD and 6PPD-Q (and other TRCs) is also essential to understand environmental exposure data. Moreover, due to rapid metabolism, it is important to measure the metabolite forms of 6PPD and 6PPD-Q (and other TRCs) in order to assess the exposure to these chemicals in wild-caught fish. ,

3.3.3. Main Individual TRCs in English Sole Bile and Plasma

Individual TRC mean concentrations per site and their percent detected are provided in TableA–B.

Among the TRC-PDs measured, 6PPD, IPPD, and 8PPD were the most frequently detected, with 6PPD being the most abundant TRC. 4-HDPA was the second highest abundant TRC in bile samples, with the maximum concentration ranging from 3.4 to 7.1 among urban sites. However, this TRC-PD’s transformation product was not detected in any of the plasma samples. Other studies also reported 6PPD and IPPD as the most commonly detected TRC-PDs. Specifically, 6PPD consistently showed the highest concentration across a variety of samples, including river and estuary sediments, air particles, water runoff, and roadside soils. The high susceptibility to degradation in the environment, also demonstrated by the stability test in this study (see Supporting Information), is another confounding factor to quantify 6PPD in environmental samples. However, even with a known short half-life in the environment, 6PPD has been detected in almost 100% of the samples in a variety of matrices. This is particularly concerning because 6PPD is the precursor to the toxic compound 6PPD-Q. Limited published data are available on the presence of 4-HDPA, a relevant TRC-PD found in this study, in tissue or plasma samples. Some of these TRC-PDs could be potentially selected by the tire industry as substitutes for 6PPD. Therefore, it is important to start baseline monitoring for several of these chemicals in aquatic wildlife.

TRC-Qs had a relatively low detection frequency. IPPD-Q and 6PPD-Q had the highest detection rates for bile and plasma. IPPD-Q and 6PPD-Q had maximum concentration levels of 1.8 ng/mL (CB site) and 0.46 ng/mL (DU site) in the bile samples, respectively. In plasma samples, these levels reached up to 0.11 ng/mL and 0.07 ng/mL, both from the EH site. The urban site DU also had relatively high levels of CPPD-Q, reaching 0.41 ng/mL in bile. Studies also reported 6PPD-Q and IPPD-Q as the most detectable TRC-Qs in different matrices. , CPPD-Q was also reported in 100% of air particles, water runoff, and roadside soil samples. Although 77PD-Q was reported as the most dominant TRC-Q in biota, in this study, it was only found in relatively low concentrations and low detection frequency in both bile and plasma.

TRC-VCRs had relatively high levels in bile samples when compared to other TRCs, particularly TRC-Qs, with DPG and HMMM being detected in 81 and 41% of the bile samples and at concentrations of up to 2.3 ng/mL (DU site) and 1.5 ng/mL (CB site), respectively. Because of an interfering chemical present in all English sole field plasma samples, DPG was not reported. In contrast to plasma samples used during method development, all plasma collected from field sites had a chromatogram peak with the same precursor/product (ion, m/z) transition that corresponded to DPG and interfered with quantitation. We speculate that the interference was most likely a component of the anticoagulant (heparin salt) used during blood sampling. HMMM was one of the most abundant TRCs in plasma samples, detected in 61% of the samples. These TRC-VCRs are widely used in industrial products, including the vehicle tire industry, which is reflected in the high detection frequency in English sole bile and plasma throughout Puget Sound. However, there is a lack of data regarding these TRC-VCRs in marine environments. Both HMMM and DPG, as well as their transformation products, have been detected in air samples from several highly urbanized cities worldwide, with DPG estimated to be the most abundant chemical. Surface water samples have been reported with levels of HMMM and DPG. ,−

3.3.4. Bile versus Plasma

A mean sum TRC concentration in bile/concentration in plasma (bile/plasma) ratio was calculated for all sites and grouped as site types (Table 6S). The mean for the summed TRC bile/plasma ratio for urban, near-urban, and nonurban sites was respectively 24, 12, and 5. The more urbanized the site, the more concentrated these chemicals were in bile, relative to plasma levels. The higher levels of TRCs in bile are most likely due to the route of metabolism and excretion, as these exogenous chemicals may accumulate in bile before the final elimination process. Due to its less complex nature and tendency to accumulate contaminants such as TRCs, fish bile is a valuable matrix for assessing fish exposure to the environmental levels of TRCs in Puget Sound, WA, USA, or even lower levels of these chemicals in other similarly urbanized areas worldwide.

The bile/plasma ratio calculated for each of the TRC groups was 2–130 for TRC-PDs, 2–8 for TRC-Qs, and 1–20 for TRC-VCRs. These ratios show a much higher presence of TRC-PDs in bile relative to TRC-Qs, but in plasma, this difference is substantially reduced. Figure illustrates the percent distribution of each TRC group and individual TRC in bile and plasma for each of the three site types. The charts for each individual site are also provided in the Supporting Information (Figure 7S). These charts illustrate the higher relative presence of TRC-Qs in plasma when compared to bile samples. 6PPD-Q followed a similar partition pattern and was found in a relatively higher percentage in plasma samples compared to bile samples. Potential differences in the kinetics of distribution and elimination between these types of TRCs within the fish might explain the differences in the relative distribution observed in bile and plasma. Understanding the toxicokinetic differences between TRC-PDs and TRC-Qs is crucial for comprehending the fate of these chemicals and for interpreting the environmental exposure levels measured in fish.

There was no correlation observed on TRC levels in bile versus plasma for either summed TRCs or any individual TRC levels, with the exception of 6PPD (r 2 > 0.5). Low levels of several TRCs in plasma, many below LOQ, create a challenge for this correlation. Toxicokinetic differences among TRCs associated with bioavailability and elimination may contribute to this lack of correlation. This study also measured only the parent TRC and did not assess the metabolites of these chemicals. Based on studies with other contaminants in fish ,, and 6PPDQ, it is possible that extensive phase I (e.g., hydroxylated) and phase II (e.g., glucuronide and sulfate conjugates) metabolism may occur. Therefore, total TRC (parent + metabolites) levels, especially in bile, may be underestimated. The environmental toxicological relevance of the data in this study cannot be currently addressed due to significant data gaps on biological end points associated with TRC levels in bile and plasma. Laboratory exposure studies are essential to closing these data gaps. Additionally, long-term monitoring across multiple species in Puget Sound would help establish temporal trends and allow for the correlation of bile and plasma levels to other extensive biological measurements taken by regional monitoring programs. Such correlations are necessary to understand the potential fish health risks associated with the levels of exposure that are currently presented.

3.4. Biological Implications

This study developed and validated a method for simultaneously quantifying environmentally relevant concentrations of several TRCs in fish bile and plasma, allowing for biological exposure measures in field-caught fish that could potentially be applied elsewhere and in other species. The suitability of this method for assessing biologically meaningful exposure is demonstrated by higher measured TRCs in urban than nonurban sites around Puget Sound, WA, USA for both bile and plasma samples and the relatively high detection rate in bile samples. Also, because the TRC levels in bile were generally an order of magnitude higher than plasma levels from the same individuals, bile may be a more valuable matrix for assessing biological exposure to low concentrations of TRCs. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first data set reporting environmental levels of these 18 TRCs in fish bile and plasma. The distribution of several TRCs in a benthic fish helps to close the gap on the environmental fate of these chemicals, since most data report TRCs in abiotic samples. However, this study did not assess potential seasonal variations in the TRC exposure or differences in exposure from different species. The long-term measurement of these chemicals in aquatic organisms is of great importance for better understanding the exposure levels and potential ecological risks.

Assessing the toxicological effects of TRCs measured in tissue samples is challenging because, to date, only acute toxicity threshold data are available for most TRCs, while sublethal chronic toxicity studies are only reported for 6PPD-Q. Furthermore, all toxicity threshold concentrations are only reported in water (μg/L).

In order to compare the toxicity thresholds with this study, we used the plasma concentration obtained from English sole and assumed a pseudoequilibrium between plasma levels and water. However, because of factors such as high dynamic exposure from stormwater events and the potential rapid metabolism of some TRCs (particularly TRC-Qs), this comparison is only a rough screening estimate.

TRCs appear to have acute toxicity levels over 1000× higher than the levels measured in the plasma of English sole. The notable exception is 6PPD-Q with its well-documented low LC50 of <0.1 μg/L in sensitive salmonid species. For some of the most detected TRC-PDs in this study, the 96 h LC50 reported for different fish species ranged between 340 and 430 μg/L for IPPD, 140–737 μg/L for 6PPD, and >61 μg/L for 8PPD. In contrast, the mean level of TRC-PDs in plasma from urban sites in this study ranged between 0.061 and 0.091 μg/L for IPPD, 0.02–0.11 μg/L for 6PPD, and 0.038–0.085 μg/L for 8PPD. The reported acute toxicity threshold for the most detected TRC-Qs was >10 μg/L for IPPD-Q and 0.04–0.095 μg/L for 6PPD-Q. The mean TRC-Q concentrations in plasma measured in fish from urban sites in this study ranged between 0.02 and 0.032 and 0.014–0.019 μg/L for IPPD-Q and 6PPD-Q, respectively. In this study, TRC-VCR HMM was detected at a high frequency in plasma samples, and its mean concentration ranged between 0.044 and 0.14 μg/L; however, HMMM has reported acute toxicity in the mg/L levels. These observations highlight the need for additional toxicity studies of TRCs at much lower test concentrations using sublethal end points.

Although the concentrations of TRC measured in English sole plasma were well below the reported acute toxicity thresholds, the measured concentrations of 6PPD-Q were closer to the range of concentrations associated with sublethal chronic effects. A few studies have reported sublethal (chronic) toxicity thresholds for 6PPD-Q, which can reach as low as 0.06 μ/L of water, leading to embryonic malformation in rainbow trout. Vascular leakage and altered cellular gene expression were observed in coho salmon exposed to 6PPD-Q as low as 0.1 μg/L of water. Although fish are usually exposed to multiple TRCs presented in stormwater, there is no empirically proven synergistic effect studies of multiple PPD-quinones. Due to a potentially similar toxicological mechanism pathway, it is expected their sublethal and lethal effects could be potentialized when combined.

In order to better understand the distribution of these TRCs in fish, biological monitoring needs to be conducted using different species including pelagic fish. Future monitoring studies would also clearly benefit from a better understanding of the toxicokinetic behavior of TRCs in fish.
